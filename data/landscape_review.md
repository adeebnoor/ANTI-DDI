# The remaining niche for a curated drug–drug interaction negative-control resource, 2026

*A systematic landscape survey conducted via Europe PMC, Crossref, arXiv and targeted web search. OpenAlex was not queried: no API key was available in this session, and keyless querying is not permitted. Every claim below traces to a record retrieved during this survey; DOIs and PMIDs are given inline and in the reference list. Where a claim could not be verified from a retrieved source, this is stated explicitly.*

## (i) What exists now

The DDI data landscape in 2026 is dense on the positive side and almost empty on the negative side.

**Positive-only clinical knowledge bases have grown substantially.** DDInter launched with roughly 0.24 M DDI associations across 1,833 approved drugs (10.1093/nar/gkab880); DDInter 2.0 now covers 2,310 drugs with 302,516 DDI records, 8,398 distinct mechanism descriptions, and additional drug–food, drug–disease and therapeutic-duplication records (10.1093/nar/gkae726). MecDDI adds mechanism clarification for curated DDIs (10.1021/acs.jcim.2c01656). None of these assert non-interaction. Absence of a pair from DDInter means "not curated", not "curated as safe" — an open-world assumption that downstream machine learning routinely violates.

**Spontaneous-report resources are positive-only by construction.** TWOSIDES contains 868,221 significant associations across 59,220 drug pairs and 1,301 adverse events, with a further 3,782,910 PRR-elevated associations; OFFSIDES holds 438,801 off-label effects for 1,332 drugs (10.1126/scitranslmed.3003377). Critically, the nsides v0.1 release notes state that the distributed files contain no information for conditions that did not occur or for drug pairs that caused no adverse events. The negative cells are not merely unlabelled — they are absent from the release. The project page itself notes the resources are substantially out of date.

**CRESCENDDI is the closest comparator and the one to position against explicitly.** Published in *Scientific Data* (10.1038/s41597-022-01159-y), it contains 10,286 positive and 4,544 negative controls covering 454 drugs and 179 adverse events mapped to RxNorm and MedDRA, plus 12,141 single-drug negative controls. Its negatives are constructed by randomly pairing drugs and events, then retaining a triplet only if the drug pair cannot be found in the intersected compendia (BNF, Thesaurus, Micromedex) *and* a templated PubMed query — `(DRUG_1) AND (DRUG_2) AND ((EVENT) OR (interaction))` — returns no results. Negatives were sampled until the number with non-zero FAERS counts matched the equivalent positive subset (N = 4,544; 435 drugs, 161 events).

Three things about CRESCENDDI define the space we can still occupy, and all three are stated by its own authors. First, the PubMed step was intended to provide confidence "about the absence of literature evidence of a potential DDI ... rather than definitive evidence to support the lack of a potential association". Second, technical validation deliberately targeted the pipeline rather than the truth of individual controls, because of what the authors call "the inability to generate 'gold standards' in pharmacovigilance". Third — and this is the structural point — CRESCENDDI's unit is a drug–drug–**event** triplet built for evaluating disproportionality signal-detection algorithms on FAERS. It is not a pair-level assertion that two drugs may be safely co-prescribed, and it was never framed as a specificity benchmark for DDI prediction models.

The same group then showed that reference-set composition is itself a confounder: varying design criteria (event background prevalence, restriction to designated medical events, evidence level) changed AUC and PPV estimates in opposing directions and by different magnitudes across three signal-detection algorithms, "potentially altering the conclusions regarding which methodologies are perceived to perform best" (10.1002/pds.5609). That paper is an argument for standardising control selection which no one has since acted on.

**HODDI is the newest dataset and the clearest illustration of the problem.** Built from FAERS 2014Q3–2024Q3, it holds 109,744 records over 2,506 drugs and 4,569 side effects, released as 41 quarterly positive/negative file pairs (arXiv:2502.06274; 10.48550/arXiv.2502.06274). Its negatives are explicitly synthetic: a positive record is taken and *both* a drug and the side effect are replaced with random alternatives, on the stated assumption that a randomly assembled combination is extremely unlikely to cause a specific side effect, with the sole constraint of non-membership in the positive set, balanced 1:1. Because positives and negatives differ in both drug identity and event, a classifier can separate them on marginal frequency alone. HODDI is a peer-reviewed-in-name-only preprint that nonetheless will be used as a benchmark.

**Regulatory-style negative controls exist, but only for single drugs.** The OMOP reference set defined 165 positive and 234 negative drug–outcome controls across four outcomes via literature review plus NLP of product labelling (10.1007/s40264-013-0097-8). A 2025 Japanese set used the Risk Management Plan for positives and *absence from the package insert* for negatives, yielding 127 positive and 386 negative controls over 43 drugs and 15 events (10.1007/s43441-024-00729-z). Both confirm that "absent from the label" remains the accepted regulatory-grade negative criterion in 2025 — and both are drug–event, not drug–drug.

## (ii) How negatives are currently handled in DDI machine learning, and why it is inadequate

The 2026 PRISMA systematic review of computational DDI methods (10.3389/fphar.2026.1816394) states the position plainly: binary and multi-type DDI tasks are highly imbalanced because unknown pairs vastly outnumber known interactions, and "most graph-based works therefore construct explicit negative samples by selecting non-edge pairs", naming MSResG, GMIA, TP-DDI, CAGPool and AutoDDI. Some studies use degree- or similarity-based heuristics to avoid trivial negatives, "but these details are often under-specified". The same review names label quality assessment and standardised evaluation protocols as underexplored, and closes by calling for transparent dataset construction and realistic evaluation protocols. A parallel critical review of 24 DDI ML studies (10.3389/fphar.2025.1632775) lists class imbalance first among the field's limitations — but treats it as a sampling nuisance to be fixed with SMOTE-style rebalancing, not as a label-validity problem.

Two independent lines of evidence establish that this is not a cosmetic concern.

The mechanistic argument comes from a 2025 *BMC Biology* study of negative-sampling bias in scale-free biomolecular networks (10.1186/s12915-025-02231-w). It finds that emphasis on novel ML approaches has "often result[ed] in overoptimistic predictive estimates", that model training and evaluation are profoundly influenced by network topology, and that degree structure alone can distort performance assessment; the authors propose degree-distribution-balanced sampling as a corrective. Drug-interaction graphs are scale-free in exactly this way — a small number of heavily annotated drugs (warfarin, amiodarone, clarithromycin) carry most edges — so a model trained against random non-edges can achieve high AUROC by learning drug popularity rather than pharmacology.

The empirical argument comes from adjacent fields where the correction has already been made. The foundational compound–protein work showed that all six classifiers tested performed markedly better on screened "highly credible" negatives than on random negatives (10.1093/bioinformatics/btv256). In protein–protein interaction prediction, topology-driven non-interaction sampling is now treated as a first-class methodological contribution, motivated explicitly by scarcity of quality hard negatives and shortcut learning (10.1093/bioinformatics/btaf148). DDI prediction has no equivalent. Its most recent benchmarking effort, DDI-Ben (10.1093/bioinformatics/btaf569), fixes the *split* — showing most of 10 representative methods degrade substantially under new-drug distribution shift — while leaving the negative *label* untouched.

The consequence is measurable and largely unmeasured: current DDI benchmarks report AUROC/AUPR against negatives whose validity nobody has checked, so a reported gain may reflect better exploitation of degree structure rather than better interaction prediction. No public DDI resource today lets a reviewer distinguish the two.

## (iii) The clinical alert-specificity motivation

The clinical case for a negative set is now stronger than the computational one, because the 2025 evidence shows DDI alerting has stopped delivering.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 studies found pooled DDI-alert override prevalence by physicians of **90% (95% CI 85–95%)**, against an alert-generation prevalence of 13% (95% CI 5–24%) (10.1177/14604582241263242, PMID 38899788). Both pooled estimates carry I² = 100%, so heterogeneity is extreme and the figure should be cited with that caveat rather than as a point estimate. A 3-year single-centre study found 73.3% of allergy, DDI and duplicate-drug alerts overridden, with only about 60% of overrides judged appropriate, and certain categories inappropriately overridden more than 75% of the time (10.1093/jamia/ocx115). Across five international EHR systems, override rates for high-priority alerts requiring provider action ranged 56.7%–83.3% (10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2017.12.027). In a prospective German evaluation, of 647 validated CDSS alerts only 82.7% were content-appropriate, and of those only 19.6% were patient-relevant (10.3389/fphar.2025.1510425).

Two 2025 studies close the argument on outcomes. A JAMIA systematic review of 8 controlled studies (N = 43,413) found DDI alerts modestly influenced prescribing (OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.01–4.27) but produced no significant reduction in targeted adverse drug interactions (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.56–1.34) and no mortality benefit; the authors conclude that optimising alert accuracy and clinical relevance is essential (10.1093/jamia/ocaf139). An Australian quasi-experimental study across five hospitals found untailored "major contraindicated" alerts reduced potential DDIs (AOR 0.38, 95% CI 0.19–0.78) but produced no change in clinically relevant DDIs (AOR 1.12, 0.68–1.84) or DDI-related harm, and advised organisations to reconsider DDI alert implementation where tailoring is not possible (10.1136/bmjqs-2024-018243).

The reason tailoring is hard is that the compendia disagree about what is not an interaction. Across BNF (51,481 pairs), Thesaurus (38,037) and Micromedex (65,446), only 6,970 pairs are common to all three — 13.54%, 18.32% and 10.65% of each respectively (10.1111/bcp.15341). A 2026 systematic review of 44 studies of DDI checkers found inter-rater reliability generally low, with Fleiss' κ from −0.155 to 0.483 and Gwet's AC1 0.12–0.46, and noted that negative κ values indicate oppositional classification rather than chance disagreement (10.1111/bph.70515). Single-compendium absence is therefore not evidence of non-interaction — a multi-source consensus criterion is required.

LLMs make this worse before they make it better. In a 2026 cross-sectional study of 186 antiseizure-medication comedication pairs against Lexicomp, ChatGPT-4 achieved sensitivity 0.842 with specificity **0.358**, and DeepSeek-V3 sensitivity 0.877 with specificity **0.200**, versus Drugs.com at 0.870/0.629; iterative prompting on false positives lifted specificity to 0.77 and 0.42 respectively (10.1016/j.rcsop.2026.100733). The authors attribute the low specificity to overclassification of non-interacting or minor pairs, and connect it directly to alert fatigue. This is, as far as this survey could establish, the only published LLM DDI evaluation that reports specificity on a defined non-interacting arm — and its negative arm is 60 pairs from one drug class judged by one commercial compendium.

## (iv) The precise niche remaining in 2026

Nothing in the retrieved literature occupies the following position, and it is defensible on all four sides:

**A curated, versioned, pair-level negative-control set in which each candidate non-interacting pair carries (a) multi-compendium concordant absence, (b) an explicit absence-of-literature-evidence audit, (c) a mechanistic implausibility assessment, and — the load-bearing novelty — (d) a quantified real-world co-exposure denominator establishing that the pair is co-prescribed often enough for an interaction to have been observed had one existed.**

Element (d) is what converts "absence of evidence" into graded "evidence of absence", and it is the element every prior resource lacks. CRESCENDDI's negatives are literature-absent but co-exposure-unquantified beyond a FAERS non-zero-count sampling constraint; HODDI's are randomly synthesised; TWOSIDES simply omits the cells; DDInter never asserts absence. Population-scale co-exposure counting is demonstrably feasible: a 2024 *BMC Medicine* study measured co-administration of known-interacting drugs in 12.51% (Blumenau), 12.12% (Indianapolis) and 10.06% (Catalonia) of individuals across three health systems (10.1186/s12916-024-03384-1). The same machinery, inverted, yields the denominator for a negative control. Label-text mining at the required scale is also solved: OnSIDES extracts >3.6 M drug–ADE pairs from 47,211 labels at F1 0.90 (10.1016/j.medj.2025.100642) — the same approach applied to DDI sections would harvest explicit "no clinically significant interaction was observed" statements, which are positive assertions of absence rather than mere silence.

The methodological standard the resource must meet is already set. A systematic review of 184 pharmacoepidemiologic studies using negative controls found that many failed to check the underlying causality assumption of the control, and identified only two reference sets with structured steps to do so (10.1093/aje/kwad201). Meeting that bar — publishing the assumption check, not just the list — is the differentiator.

**Reframe the claim accordingly.** Do not publish "a list of safe drug combinations": that claim is clinically indefensible (no dataset can license co-prescription), it is what the retracted paper effectively claimed, and reviewers will reject it. Publish instead: **a specificity benchmark for DDI prediction and alerting, consisting of drug pairs with graded, quantified evidence of non-interaction, together with a reference evaluation protocol that reports specificity and false-positive rate alongside the AUROC/AUPR that current benchmarks report alone.** Under that framing the deliverable is a *benchmark with an evidence layer*, each pair carries a confidence tier rather than a binary safe/unsafe flag, and the validity argument rests on transparent, auditable criteria rather than on an unprovable claim of true negativity. Kontsioti et al. already demonstrated that reference-set composition changes which algorithm looks best (10.1002/pds.5609); a resource that standardises the negative arm is the direct response to that finding.

Note also that the retraction is recoverable ground rather than a liability. Europe PMC records three citations of PMID 35437468, of which one is the retraction notice itself and one is a self-citation by the same authors; the single independent scientific citation is a *GigaScience* review (10.1093/gigascience/giad011) which, under a section headed "Building a negative dataset of DDIs with drug pairs known as noninteracting in specific clinical circumstances", argues that negative DDI information is scarce, that finding it means navigating a vast search space, and that tools for predicting non-interacting drug pairs would be highly beneficial. That is an independent, citable endorsement of the niche whose argument does not depend on the retracted data. The rebuild should cite the retraction transparently in the manuscript rather than let a reviewer discover it.

## (v) Journal fit

**Recommended: *Scientific Data*.**

The resource is a dataset with a construction pipeline and a technical-validation section, which is precisely the Data Descriptor form. CRESCENDDI established that this exact subject matter is in scope at this journal (10.1038/s41597-022-01159-y), and the "Technical Validation" convention lets us do what the field needs — publish the auditable criteria and the assumption checks — without having to claim gold-standard truth, which CRESCENDDI's authors themselves declared impossible in pharmacovigilance. Publishing alongside the closest comparator also makes the delta legible to reviewers: same venue, adjacent problem, explicitly different unit of analysis (pair-level with co-exposure evidence, versus triplet-level literature absence). Mandatory open deposition and versioning further distance the work from the retracted original.

*JAMIA* is the second choice and the right home for a **companion** paper, not the resource itself. Its 2025 systematic review concluding that DDI alerts do not improve patient-important outcomes and that alert accuracy must be optimised (10.1093/jamia/ocaf139) shows the journal owns the alert-specificity problem; a paper that applies the resource to measure the false-positive rate of deployed DDI checkers, or of LLM-based checkers, would fit there. But JAMIA expects an informatics evaluation with a result, not a data descriptor.

*Drug Safety* is third. It is the natural venue for the pharmacovigilance reference-set tradition — it published the OMOP reference set (10.1007/s40264-013-0097-8) — and would welcome a signal-detection-facing negative-control set. Its weakness for this specific submission is that its readership is pharmacovigilance rather than DDI machine learning, and the strongest part of our claim is the ML specificity benchmark; the *Scientific Data* route reaches both audiences.

- **Google Scholar / Scopus / Dimensions citation counts of the retracted paper were not checked.** The count of 3 (of which 1 is the retraction notice and 1 a self-citation) is Europe PMC's index only and is a lower bound.
